# The Seventh Noon

The loading yard behind Harlan Paper Supply smelled like hot garbage and old blood.

Evan Cross stood at the corner of the fence and counted. Three of them. They were crouched around something on the concrete near the second dock door, and from the sound, they were eating it.

*Three. Fine. Three is fine.*

A week ago they had been people. A man in a gray work shirt. A big woman in a hospital uniform. A third one so swollen he could not tell what it used to wear. Now they were all wider than they should be, heavy in the shoulders, their skin pulled tight and shiny over new muscle. The fed ones always looked like that. They ate, and they grew, and they got smarter about doors.

He looked at the sky. The sun was high. Almost noon.

*The voice said the seventh noon. That's today. I'd like to be home with a can of beans for it, not stuck in a paper company yard.*

He stepped out from the fence.

The swollen one lifted its head first. It had no eyes left, only dark holes, but it turned straight to him anyway. Then the other two. Their mouths were red to the chin.

"Hi," Evan said. "Don't get up."

They got up.

The first one came in a rush, the way they always did. Nothing, then everything. It crossed twenty feet of concrete in a blink, arms wide, mouth open.

Evan let the lightning come.

It started in his chest, a tight hot buzz like a wire pulled too far, and then it ran down his arms and out over his skin. The hair on his arms stood up. Blue light crawled over his knuckles. The air smelled sharp and clean for one second, the way it does before a storm.

He stepped into the rush instead of away from it. His right hand hit the man in the gray shirt in the center of the chest.

*Crack.*

The sound was like a tree branch breaking. The zombie's body went stiff in the air, every muscle locked, and it dropped at his feet and did not move. Smoke came out of its shirt.

The big woman in the hospital uniform was already there. She was faster than she looked. Her hand caught his jacket at the shoulder and pulled, and for a second Evan felt how strong she was. She was going to rip his arm off the way a kid pulls a leg off a toy.

*Nope.*

He pushed the lightning into his legs.

The world went slow and soft. He tore free, leaving a piece of jacket in her fist, and he was behind her in three steps. Three steps that took no time at all. He saw her head start to turn. Too slow. He put his palm flat on the back of her skull and let go.

The bang was loud enough to ring off the warehouse walls. Something wet hit the side of his face. She fell forward and slid a little on the concrete.

The swollen one had not charged. It stood by the dock door, head tilted, listening.

*That one's the smart one.*

It backed up one step. Then another. It was going for the dark gap under the dock door.

"No you don't," Evan said.

He raised his hand and threw.

The bolt left his fingers with a sound like tearing cloth. A white line jumped across the yard, about ten steps, and hit the thing in the throat. It jerked, arms flying out, and it went down hard against the steel door with a boom that echoed through the whole empty building behind it.

Then it was quiet.

Evan stood there, breathing. His hands shook a little. The buzz under his skin faded slowly, and in its place came the hunger, a deep empty pull in his stomach like he had not eaten in two days. He knew this part. He hated this part.

*Three in about ten seconds. Not bad. I'm getting good at this. That should worry me more than it does.*

He walked over to the swollen one. Its chest was still smoking. Next to its open hand, on the concrete, something caught the light.

A small crystal, the size of a fingertip. Blue. Hard and cold when he picked it up.

"There you are," he said.

He put it in his pocket with the other two. Three now. Small ones, but small ones added up.

He looked down at what they had been eating near the dock door. It had been a dog. A big one. Some of it was still bigger than any dog should be.

*Even the dogs are growing. Great.*

He wiped his face with his sleeve and left.

---

The walk home took twenty minutes. He knew the way without thinking. Before all this, he had carried packages to every street in Sable on a motorcycle, eight years of front doors, back doors, loading docks and angry people who would not sign. He knew where the alleys went. He knew which streets ran downhill.

Now the streets were empty.

Cars sat where they had stopped seven days ago, doors open, some with the keys still inside. A bus lay on its side on Canal Street with grass already pushing up around its wheels. A woman's shoe sat in the middle of the road. Crows walked on the hoods of cars like they owned them. Maybe they did now.

The flies were the worst part. You heard them before you saw what they were on.

"Nice day," Evan said to a crow. "Real nice. Sunny."

The crow looked at him and did not answer. Smart crow.

Riverside Self Storage stood at the end of the street by the water, four stories of gray concrete with no windows on the ground floor. A tall chain fence ran around the lot. He pulled open the side gate, chained it again behind him, and crossed the lot past the rolling steel gate he never opened. Inside, the halls were rows of orange roll-up doors, all shut. His boots sounded loud on the floor.

He took the east stairwell to the top. Fourth floor, unit 418, the big one at the end. A mattress on the floor. A camping stove. Cans stacked against the wall. Six jugs of water. A stolen dirt bike in the corner that he had not started in three days because the sound carried too far.

He grabbed a can of beans and a spoon and went up the last flight to the roof.

---

The roof was flat and hot under the sun, with a low wall around the edge. From here he could see half the city.

He sat with his back to the air vent, opened the can with his knife, and ate cold beans while he looked.

Smoke. That was the first thing anybody would see. Three tall black lines to the east, where something had been burning for days and nobody was ever going to put it out. A gray haze over the old town. Further north, the National Guard armory, quiet now in the daylight. At night there was always gunfire up there.

To the south, the Harbor Bridge stretched over the gray bay. Even from here he could see the cars pushed across both ends to make walls. Somebody had a lot of people over there. Somebody with guns, who shot anyone who came close. At night their lights burned like the bridge was still part of the old world.

*Someone's building a kingdom over there. A week in, and someone's already a king.*

He had seen enough this week to know how this would go. The strong had already started taking from the weak. A woman jumping roof to roof like the street was nothing. A man on Fifth Street setting a bus on fire with his bare hands while people screamed inside it. Evan had not stopped to see who.

He was not going to be one of the weak ones. That was the whole plan. That was the only plan.

He scraped the last beans out of the can.

*So. The voice. "I will speak again at the seventh noon." What do you say after "most of you will be hungry"? Good job? Here's a prize?*

He tried to imagine it. Rules, maybe. A reason. An apology. Some kind of help. He laughed once at that, out loud.

Nobody was waiting for him to come home safe. Nobody had ever been waiting, really. He had grown up in five foster homes, and in every one he had learned the same thing: keep your own food, keep your own key, and don't count on anyone to come back. It made him good at this.

He checked his watch. The battery still worked. 11:58.

The flies buzzed somewhere below. A crow called. The bay moved slowly against the stones.

11:59.

*Okay. Talk to me.*

12:00.

The voice came into his head the way it had seven days ago. Calm. Clear. Not loud and not soft. It did not come from any direction. It was simply there, inside, like his own thoughts, except it was not him.

[The first week is over.]

[Most of you are gone. The rest of you have learned.]

[These are the ten strongest in this city:]

Evan sat up.

[Victor Crane.]

[Mei Lin Zhou.]

[Diego Santos.]

[Hannah Volk.]

[Tobias Grant.]

[Daniel Okoye.]

[Evan Cross.]

His heart stopped for one beat. Then it started again, too hard.

[Samuel Price.]

[Nadia Petrova.]

[Ava Torres.]

[Kill one of them, and what they carry is yours.]

[I will speak again at the thirtieth noon.]

Then it was gone, and the roof was very quiet.

---

Evan did not move.

*Evan Cross.*

His name. His real name, the one on his license and on the jacket of every foster file. Said in that calm voice into the head of every living person in Sable at the same time.

Seventh on the list.

*Seventh strongest. In the whole city.*

For one second, something warm and proud came up in his chest. He had worked for it. Nine cores crushed. A week of hunting while other people hid. He was good at this, and now something had said so.

Then the next line came back to him, and the warm feeling went cold.

*Kill one of them, and what they carry is yours.*

What he carried. His lightning. His eyes. Everything he had built this week. Anyone who killed him would get it. Not a small blue core. All of it.

He was not a person anymore. He was a prize.

Far away, the city answered.

First it was shouts. Thin and faint, carried on the wind from the north, too far to understand. Many voices at once, like a crowd. Then a burst of gunfire from the same direction, fast and long, the sound of somebody emptying a gun. It stopped all at once. It did not start again.

Then, from the south, a horn. Long and deep, from the Harbor Bridge. It blew once, and then again, and then a third time.

*That's a signal. They're calling their people in. Or sending them out.*

Evan stood up and looked across the city. All those roofs. All those broken windows. Somewhere out there were people like the man who burned the bus. People who had heard ten names just now and were thinking the same thing he was.

He made himself think slowly.

*Okay. Facts.*

*Everyone knows my name. Everyone.*

*Nobody knows my face.* He had no friends left alive in this city. No family. His manager was the first thing he ever killed. The people who knew him from the courier company were mostly dead or eating each other. Nobody could point at him and say, that one.

*Unless I tell them. So I don't tell anybody my name. Ever.*

*And the other nine.* He did not know a single one of those names. Victor Crane. Mei Lin Zhou. Hannah Volk. They meant nothing to him. But they were out there, and they were stronger than everyone else, and six of them were stronger than him.

*And every strong person out there who didn't make the list just got told how to get there. Kill one of the ten. Take it all.*

He looked down at his hands. Blue sparks moved over his knuckles without him asking them to.

"Great," he said to nobody. "Really great. Thanks a lot."

He sat back down. He did not finish his water. He watched the streets.

---

He stayed on the roof for most of an hour, watching. Nothing moved in the warehouse district except crows. The smoke kept rising in the east. The horn on the bridge did not blow again.

He took the three cores out of his pocket and looked at them in his palm. Small, blue, cold.

*If I'm going to be a prize, I'd better be an expensive one.*

He was about to close his hand when a voice came up from the street.

"Evan Cross!"

He froze.

It was a woman's voice. Clear and loud, from below, near the front gate.

His full name. Spoken out loud, in the open, at the building where he slept.

*No. Nobody knows. Nobody can know.*

He shoved the cores back into his pocket and dropped low. He moved along the roof wall, bent over, until he was above the front of the lot. Then he looked over the edge, slowly.

Below, on the other side of the steel gate, a woman stood alone in the middle of the street.

She wore a black rain jacket with the hood down. Her dark hair was tied back tight. A backpack hung on her shoulders. Both of her hands were raised in front of her, open and empty, fingers spread so he could see them.

She was looking up. Right at the roof. Right at him.

Evan felt the lightning come alive under his skin, all on its own, buzzing hot down both arms.

*How. How does she know.*

The woman did not lower her hands. She did not step closer to the fence. She looked at him for a long moment, and when she spoke, her voice carried up the concrete wall clear and steady.

"My name's Ava Torres. You heard it an hour ago."
