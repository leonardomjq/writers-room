# The Wrong Pocket

Glasshouse Row was loudest at dusk.

The evening glass market filled the street at the top of the Sinks, where the stairs from the canals met the first gas lamps of the Lanes. Stalls stood shoulder to shoulder under canvas. Coins of glass sat in trays on velvet, gray and amber and a few deep blue ones under little cages, catching the light. Sellers called prices. Buyers held coins up to the lamps and squinted. The fog came up the stairs from the water and sat around everyone's ankles.

Kit Ashby walked through all of it with his hands in his pockets and his face still.

He was looking for a mind with one thing in it.

That was the trick he had worked out in nine days. The gift took whatever sat nearest the top, and he could not choose. So he chose the person instead. A man laughing with friends had a hundred small things near the top. A man chewing on one problem had only the problem.

He found one by the Holloway stall. A clerk from the Lanes, by the coat: good brown wool, a little shiny at the elbows, worth maybe a shilling and six. Bare hands, ink on the right thumb. He stood by a tray of amber glass but he was not looking at it. His lips were moving. He was counting something under his breath, over and over, and frowning at the end of it every time.

*A man who counts for a living. Maybe I learn how the Lanes keep their books.*

Kit took a penny glass from his pocket, one of Ness's cheap grays, and let it fall as he passed. It bounced once on the stones and rolled against the clerk's shoe.

The clerk bent and picked it up without thinking. People always did.

"Yours?"

"Thank you, sir." Kit took the coin back, and the man's fingers with it, a beat too long. The clerk started to pull his hand away. One. Two.

Cold ran up his arm.

He saw a tall cabinet with small drawers, a brass key turning in a lock, a column of figures that did not add up, and the clerk's own hand writing a number over another number.

Three.

Kit let go and stepped back. The man swayed. He put a hand to the back of his neck and rubbed it, and looked around as if someone had opened a window.

"Cold tonight," Kit said.

"Yes," the man said slowly. He looked at the amber glass in front of him like he did not know why he was standing there. Then he shook his head and walked off.

Kit walked the other way. The new memory sat behind his eyes beside the old one, two cold coins in a row. The woman counting her money, three days old now and getting thin. The Lanes man changing a number in a book.

*Two carried. One space left.*

He did not know yet what the clerk's number was worth. Maybe nothing. Maybe a lot, to the right buyer, if that buyer did not ask how a boy from the Sinks came to know it. He had not sold a single one yet. He kept telling himself that was patience. Some nights he knew it was fear.

The Wardens walked the Row in pairs on market nights. Kit could see two of them now at the far end, in their dark blue capes, bored. He kept a stall between him and them without thinking about it.

On the lamp post by the Holloway stall was a new bill. The paste was still wet at the corners.

> **TEN CROWNS**
> For true word leading to the taking of the person called
> **THE CANAL HOLLOWER**
> Inquiries to Warden-Inspector Varga, Lock Street Station.
> By order of the Mint.

The Mint's lantern was printed at the bottom, small and neat.

Ten crowns. Kit priced it without meaning to. Ten crowns was five years of Ness's wages. It was a room in the Lanes with a window that opened. It was more than the three people by the canals had been worth to anyone while they still knew their own names.

He moved on.

He was between the pie seller and a stall of cheap shilling glass when someone stepped in front of him and did not step aside.

She was small, smaller than him, with a sharp freckled face and a cap pulled low over stuffed-up hair. Her green coat was too big for her by two sizes. When she smiled, one front tooth was chipped.

"Evening," she said. "Busy night for you."

Kit knew her face. He had seen it on the Row all week, here and there, the way you see a pigeon. He had not thought about it once. That was a mistake, and he felt it land in his stomach before she said another word.

"I don't know you," he said.

"No. I know you, though." She leaned on the pie stall's post, easy, blocking the gap. "Pell. I work this street. Have done for two years. And for a week now I've been watching a new boy work it too, and I can't for my life see what he takes."

"I'm not taking anything."

"Sure. You bump a man, he goes white, he rubs his neck like he's been bit. You bump an old lady, same thing. Every purse still on them. I checked a couple after." She grinned. "So either you're very bad at this or you're doing something I've never seen. You keep coming back. Must be worth something."

Kit kept his face still. "I'm a doctor. I check pulses. It's charity work."

She just looked at him.

"Same street," she said. "Every dusk. Same hour. Same coat with the patch on the shoulder. You think nobody's looking because nobody's lost a purse. I'm looking. Who else is?"

Kit did not answer.

He was counting. Nine days. Eleven lifts. Most of them here, on this street, at this hour, with the same two Wardens at the end of it. He had been so pleased with himself for picking his marks well that he had never once picked his street.

*Stupid. Stupid in a way that hangs people.*

"Here's how it goes," Pell said. "You cut me in. Whatever it is, I can help. I know who looks and who doesn't, and I know who buys things nobody asks about. Or you take it somewhere else, off my street, and stop making my marks jumpy."

"And if I do neither?"

The grin got a little smaller. "Then I find out what it is by myself. And when I know what it is, I'll know what it's worth to people."

"I'll think about it," Kit said.

"Think fast." She pushed off the post and stepped back into the crowd. "Tomorrow, Ashby. Say hello to Mother Ness for me."

Then she was gone, a green coat between other coats, and Kit stood there with his own name in his ears.

He should go home. He knew it. He turned toward the stairs.

The crowd broke behind him.

Someone was pushing through it hard, the way people do when they do not care who they knock over. A tray of glass went down with a crash and a seller shouted. Kit turned.

A young man came through the gap. He was tall and thin in the arms and legs, with a flat cap and soot on one cheek. He carried a lamplighter's pole, the long one with the brass hook at the top, and he held it like he had forgotten it was in his hand. His face was white as paper. He kept looking back over his shoulder, down toward the canal stairs.

He saw Kit, of all people, and came straight at him. Maybe because Kit was standing still when no one else was.

His hand closed on Kit's bare wrist.

"Please," the lamplighter said. His voice shook. "Please, you have to... there's a man. At the lock. He put his hand on her and she just..."

Kit could have pulled away.

He did not. Half of it was the reflex nine days had built in him: skin on skin, hold still, take. The other half was simpler. A grown man was shaking like a dog in the rain, and Kit wanted to know what could do that.

One. Two.

Cold came up his arm like canal water.

He saw a narrow wet passage along the lock, the lamp just lit above it and still flickering. A woman with a laundry basket on her hip, backed against the wall. A man in a long gray coat in front of her, tall and thin, with his right hand bare and laid flat on her face, almost tenderly. Her eyes going empty. Not closing. Emptying, like a cup tipped out. And on the man's collar, caught in the new lamplight, a small silver pin.

Three.

The lamplighter let go.

He stood blinking at Kit. His hand went to the back of his neck. His mouth was still open from the word he had been saying, and he did not seem to know what it was.

"I..." He looked at the pole in his hand. He looked back toward the stairs, and he was still shaking, all over, but now his face had nothing in it but confusion. "Sorry. I'm sorry. I think I've had a turn. I left my round. I just ran. Why did I run?"

Kit could not speak for a moment.

The memory sat behind his eyes, heavier than the other two, very cold. Three coins. No space left.

*That pin was a Mint pin.*

"You should sit down," Kit said. His own voice sounded far away.

"Yes. Yes, I should." The lamplighter laughed, a bad, ashamed sound. "I must've drunk something. God. Don't tell my foreman."

The crowd was already closing around them, losing interest. The fallen tray was being picked up. Kit took one step back from the lamplighter, then another.

And the man in gray walked into the Row.

He came up the canal stairs at an easy walking pace, and the crowd moved for him the way water moves for a boat. He was tall and thin, with neat graying hair and a clean-shaven face, and a long gray coat cut better than anything in the Sinks. Black gloves, like a Rings man. On his collar was the Mint's small silver lantern.

He looked kind. That was the worst part. He looked like someone's uncle come to fetch them home.

He walked straight to the lamplighter.

"There you are," he said softly. "You left your round."

The lamplighter turned and looked at him with no fear at all. Kit had taken the fear. It was sitting behind Kit's eyes.

The man in gray drew off his right glove, one finger at a time. Then he laid his bare hand along the young man's cheek, the way you would touch a sick child.

The lamplighter went stiff. His back straightened. His eyes stared at nothing.

Kit could not breathe.

The man in gray stayed like that for a few seconds. Then he frowned. It was a small frown, a puzzled one, the face of a man who reaches into a drawer for something and finds the drawer already open.

He took his hand away. He lifted his head and looked over the crowd.

He did it slowly, face by face, stall by stall.

Kit knew he should turn around. He knew he should look at the glass, the pies, his boots, anything. He did not move. Some part of him was sure that moving would be the thing that gave him away.

The pale eyes reached him and stopped.

They stayed. One breath. Two.

Kit held his face still, the way he had learned to hold it as a parish boy, the way he held it when Ness tried to catch him in a price. It did not help. The man was learning it by heart.

Then the man in gray smiled a little, as if they had both heard the same joke.

He drew his glove back on.

"Mint business," he said to the nearest stallholder, pleasantly, in a voice made to carry just far enough. "This young man is unwell. I'll see him looked after."

He put his gloved hand on the lamplighter's shoulder. The lamplighter walked beside him like a sleepy child, the pole still in his fist, its brass hook bobbing over the crowd.

Nobody stopped them. A Warden at the far end even touched his cap.

They went down the canal stairs into the fog, and the man in gray did not look back once.

---

Kit went home the long way.

He took the Tanner's Cut down to the water, then the plank bridge over Sallow Canal, then the back of the dye works where the steam made the alley white. He stopped twice in doorways and waited, counting to fifty, watching the way he had come. Nothing followed but fog.

*Why didn't he chase me?*

Kit's face was not hard to find. A boy in a patched coat who walked Glasshouse Row every dusk. Pell had found him in a week without even trying.

High on Mint Hill the Lantern burned white on its tower, the way it did every night, over every street. Kit had never once thought of it as an eye before.

Tallow Steps was quiet. Ness's Glass had its shutters half down, and through the window he could see Mother Ness moving behind the counter, slow and large in her shawls, putting trays away into the safe. The bell over the door gave its tired little ring when he came in.

"There's my clerk." She did not turn around. Her rings clicked on the glass trays. "Late. Did a girl finally look at you?"

"No."

She turned then, and her eyes went over him once, the way they went over a coin. The loupe on its chain rose and fell on her chest with her breathing. She coughed, wet and long, into a handkerchief.

"You're gray, Kit. Clear gray. Penny glass." She smiled. "Somebody walk over your grave?"

"Fog's thick tonight."

"Fog's always thick." She shut the safe and turned the key. "Dace comes round tomorrow night. Back door, as usual. He says his man's paying well this month for anything fresh. Anything strange." She put the key in her shawl. "And I've been counting my blanks. Two short in the drawer. Mice, I suppose. Clever mice."

She said it in the same voice as everything else. Warm. Teasing. Easy.

But her eyes stayed on him one beat after the words were done. Then another.

"Goodnight, Mother," Kit said.

"Goodnight, clerk."

---

The attic was cold and low, with a slanted roof he could touch from the bed, one small round window, and a crate for a table. He did not light the candle. He sat on the bed in his coat with his back to the wall and his knees up.

The memory sat behind his eyes, cold as a stone from the canal bottom.

He could feel all three of them. The woman counting coins, faint now, almost gone. The Lanes man and his changed number. And the lock. The gray coat. The bare hand. That one was so cold it ached.

Three days, and it would fade to nothing. Three days, and the memory he'd taken would be gone, the way it was already gone from the man who had seen it.

He found he was humming, low. The old woman's song for her baby. He stopped.

Somewhere out there the lamplighter was walking beside that man right now, or sitting in a room with him, or lying by a canal. And he was not afraid. He had no reason to be. Kit had his reason. Kit had taken it off him in two seconds, for curiosity, and then stood there while the man came for what was left.

Kit pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes.

Through the round window the Lantern's white light lay on the floorboards.

And behind his eyes, the thing itself, still sealed. If he opened it, he would know that man's face as well as the lamplighter had known it. He would be the one who had seen.

A man who emptied people had looked straight at him and smiled.

Kit took his hands from his eyes. The cold coin sat there, waiting, and the night was not going to get any longer.
