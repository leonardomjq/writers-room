# Lines that pulled a reader out of earlier stories, with why. The reader gets this list under each chapter; the
# writer never does (a writer shown a bad example copies it). The author adds a line each time the player catches a new
# kind of problem; the names in the examples belong to old stories.

- A trait from the notes turned into a wink at the reader: in the middle of the horror, the hero thinks "I've read
  this story a thousand times". Show a trait through what the person does, in the world's own terms, never by naming
  its source.
- "It was a calm voice, close as a thought, speaking in English because English was what he thought in." A note
  retold as narration, explaining what no reader asked. Better: "It was calm, and very close, and it said:".
- Someone fears something the story never showed could happen. People know and fear only what the page has shown.
- "she said it like she meant it", "said it flat, like weather". Tone tags: show what the person does instead.
- "which is the most anyone's ever paid for your stillness". A tail that explains or rates the line before it.
- "that's not a request, that's a term". A slogan: a neat, quotable line nobody says.
- "You came in person." "I came in person." An echo answer that hands the line back.
- Long narration joined by "and, and, and". Two or three plain sentences instead, each a whole action.
- Words like custodian, window dressing, up for grabs, pew, lintel. Hard or idiomatic English: the most common plain
  word instead (bench, the top of the door).
- An hour into the end of the world, the boss calls to check on the hero, every line a "gruff boss who cares" line, and
  every line about the hero. The call happened only because the story needed to hand the hero a route. Ask of every
  event: would this happen here for its own reasons? And of every line: in this person's shoes, right now, would I say
  this?
- "Not fists on steel. Softer.", "Not looted, broken.", "It wasn't a feeling. It was an instruction." Defining a thing
  by what it is not, often as a staged reveal. Say what it is, once.
- Every speech ends on a neat movie line ("so that's the one you can't break", "better you hear it now than find out
  the hard way"); a father in shock speaks in tidy sentences; a line lists facts for the reader. Real people stop
  talking without a closing line, and talk worse when they are scared.
- Two people telling each other what both just saw, then a quip to close it. The hero's humor is in how they see
  things, not in tidy lines that wrap a moment up.
- Nobody names the monsters: "it", "the thing", "those things". People give everyday names to what they see every day
  (zombies, a fed one, the big one); vague names read as fake.
- A new place named in pieces and never shown; nine people arriving at once with one detail each, so none sticks;
  comparisons the reader has to work out. Show a new place once, plainly; a few people at a time; the plain picture
  over the clever one.
- English like a literary thriller ("a hole where breakfast should have been"). Web novels say it straight: what the
  hero wants and why, how people react, a new person in a plain sentence or two.
- Runs of sentences that each hand over one more detail ("The sun stood high and white. No shade anywhere. In the dark
  he owned this place."), and endings that list the whole situation again. A detail earns a line only when it changes
  what someone does.
